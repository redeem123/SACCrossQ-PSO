\title{Soft Actor-Critic with Rank-Residual PSO Parameter Adaptation for UAV Path Planning}
\author[aff1]{Viet Anh Ngo\orcid{0009-0002-5429-8554}}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\ead{anh.nv215671@sis.hust.edu.vn}
\address[aff1]{School of Mechanical Engineering, Hanoi University of Science and Technology, No.~1 Dai Co Viet Road, Hai Ba Trung District, Hanoi, Vietnam}
\begin{abstract}
Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is attractive for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) path planning because of its simplicity and fast convergence, but its performance remains highly sensitive to parameter selection.
This paper presents RRSACPSO, a self-adaptive PSO framework built around rank-residual control and trained with a Soft Actor--Critic backbone.
A compact swarm-summary state encodes progress, diversity, and stagnation, while a simple fitness-improvement reward keeps learning aligned with the optimization objective.
The core mechanism is a 9D latent action that is expanded into particle-wise inertia, cognitive, and social coefficients through rank-residual modulation around a deterministic baseline schedule, yielding structured heterogeneity without direct full per-particle control.
In a matched four-scenario internal ablation against global, 5-subgroup, and direct per-particle SAC control interfaces, RRSACPSO attains the best mean fitness in all four cases and significantly outperforms the global and 5-subgroup variants on the three fixed-map scenarios.
Against external RL baselines on the same benchmark, it remains competitive, achieves the lowest variance in the first two fixed-map scenarios, and significantly outperforms RLAMPSO, DQN-PSO, and SAC-SAPSO, although PPO-PSO and MPSORL achieve lower mean fitness in the harder fixed-map cases.
These results are consistent with the interpretation that RRSACPSO benefits from its structured rank-residual control interface, which combines a scheduled prior with low-dimensional particle-wise adaptation and avoids unconstrained per-particle control.

\end{frontmatter} \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) support missions ranging from precision agriculture to defense, disaster response \cite{Delavarpour2021A, Fascista2022Toward, Alotaibi2019LSAR}.
Every mission hinges on path planning to compute safe trajectories through cluttered three-dimensional environments.
Despite decades of study, planning in large, uncertain airspaces remains challenging because algorithms must simultaneously reason about vehicle dynamics, environmental constraints, and mission-specific trade-offs \cite{AitSaadi2022UAV, Zhou2025A}.
Recent work on coordinated missions highlights the complexity of multiobjective multi-UAV planning, where competing mission goals must be balanced under severe environmental constraints \cite{Meng2025Multiobjective}.

Among the many available planners, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{Kennedy1995Particle} has gained broad adoption for UAV path planning because of its conceptual simplicity, fast convergence, and natural fit for continuous search spaces \cite{Cheng2024Research}.
However, PSO's performance critically depends on carefully tuned inertia and acceleration coefficients, which are notoriously problem-dependent and can cause premature convergence when left static \cite{Yang2015Lowdiscrepancy}.
While adaptive PSO variants address this issue through fitness-based or time-varying parameter schedules \cite{Bansal2011Inertia, Liu2025A}, such hand-designed rules often transfer poorly across terrains and obstacle densities and remain insensitive to changing swarm states.
Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a more principled alternative by treating parameter adaptation as a sequential decision problem.

Despite recent progress, RL-based PSO studies still leave one central question insufficiently resolved: whether performance gains come from the learning backbone itself or from how parameter control is distributed across the swarm.
Existing methods usually compare end-to-end designs that differ simultaneously in RL algorithm, state representation, reward formulation, and control interface, so the literature still lacks controlled evidence isolating the effect of control-interface granularity under matched learning conditions.
Under that broader design mismatch, global control may be too coarse to reflect particle-level search status, fixed-subgroup control imposes a predefined swarm structure, and direct per-particle control can greatly enlarge the action space and complicate learning.
Because prior comparisons usually mix these choices together, they do not support a clear causal account of whether gains come from the RL algorithm or from the control interface itself.
To address this gap, we propose RRSACPSO, a reinforcement-learning-guided PSO framework centered on rank-residual control.
The main contribution of this work is a control law that expands a compact latent action into particle-wise inertia, cognitive, and social coefficients using swarm context and particle rank, yielding structured heterogeneity without the action-space cost of direct per-particle control.
This design aims to preserve particle-wise heterogeneity without incurring the full action-space burden of direct per-particle control.

This central idea is evaluated through a matched internal comparison of global, fixed-subgroup, direct per-particle, and rank-residual SAC control interfaces under a fixed SAC backbone and shared evaluation protocol.
The online learning setup uses a compact swarm state, a fitness-improvement reward, and a retained SAC agent with twin critics, automatic entropy tuning, and target networks.
Across a four-scenario benchmark, RRSACPSO achieves the best mean fitness in all four same-backbone ablation scenarios, records the lowest run-to-run variance in the first two fixed-map RL cases, and does not achieve the best mean fitness against the strongest external RL baselines.
Against self-adaptive PSO variants, the current matched benchmark shows lower mean fitness for SAEPSO in Scenarios 1 and 4 and for SAEPSO* in Scenarios 2 and 3, although it does not support a blanket runtime-speedup claim over the strongest classical baselines.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:background} 
The UAV path planning literature is largely dominated by three paradigms: graph-search, sampling-based approaches, and population-based optimization techniques \cite{AitSaadi2022UAV, Zhou2025A}.
Classical graph-search methods such as A* and Dijkstra guarantee optimal paths on discretized maps \cite{Hart1968A}, but their cost scales poorly with map resolution and state dimensionality.
Potential-field methods react quickly online yet remain vulnerable to local minima in cluttered environments \cite{Mac2016Heuristic}.
Sampling-based planners such as RRT and PRM avoid explicit discretization and provide probabilistic completeness \cite{Karur2021A}, but often require smoothing or post-processing before they produce flyable UAV trajectories.
Hybrid approaches such as PRM-RL reduce some of these weaknesses by combining sampling with learned local navigation \cite{Faust2017PRMRL}.

Population-based optimization remains attractive when the objective directly couples path length, terrain clearance, altitude, and obstacle avoidance.
Genetic algorithms, differential evolution, and Grey Wolf Optimizer have all shown competitive UAV-planning performance under such multi-constraint objectives \cite{Debnath2024An, AitSaadi2022UAV}.
Within this family, PSO is especially popular because it is simple to implement, converges quickly in continuous spaces, and maps naturally to waypoint-based path parameterizations \cite{Cheng2024Research, Phung2021Safetyenhanced}.
Ensemble and self-adaptive PSO variants further improve robustness by varying search behaviors within the swarm \cite{Lynn2017Ensemble}.

Standard PSO updates particle velocity and position through inertia, cognitive, and social terms \cite{Kennedy1995Particle, Clerc2002The}:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
v_i^{t+1} &= w\,v_i^{t} + c_1 r_1^{t}\odot(p_i^{t}-x_i^{t}) + c_2 r_2^{t}\odot(g^{t}-x_i^{t}),\\
x_i^{t+1} &= x_i^{t}+v_i^{t+1}.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
Here, \(w\) denotes inertia, \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are the cognitive and social coefficients, and \(r_1,r_2\) are random vectors. Because PSO performance is highly sensitive to these control parameters, adaptation is central to practical deployment.

Existing PSO adaptation strategies can be grouped into time-varying schedules, fitness-based adaptation, and learning-based control.
Time-varying methods such as LDIW, TVAC, and oscillating or sinusoidal schedules are simple and computationally cheap \cite{Eberhart2000Comparing, Ratnaweera2004Selforganizing, Nickabadi2011A, Kentzoglanakis2009Particle, Chen2018A}, but they cannot react explicitly to swarm-specific stagnation or feasibility dynamics.
Fitness-based methods instead use online swarm feedback.
SAEPSO \cite{Liu2025A} adapts parameters on a per-particle basis through a normalized fitness-adaptation signal with sinusoidal temporal modulation, while UAPSO \cite{Isiet2019Selfadapting} derives its control parameters from an evolutionary-state metric.
Adaptive-state strategies such as APSO \cite{Zhan2009Adaptive} and entropy-based diversity control \cite{Han2022Improved} likewise respond to real-time convergence behavior, and fitness-based adjustment has repeatedly outperformed static parameter schedules on benchmark problems \cite{Li2017Particle}.
However, convergence analyses also show that self-adapted parameters do not automatically satisfy stable convergence conditions \cite{Harrison2018Selfadaptive}, which leaves open the question of how to adapt PSO parameters in a state-aware yet robust manner.

Reinforcement learning (RL) casts PSO parameter control as a sequential decision problem in which the controller observes swarm statistics, chooses parameter updates, and receives rewards tied to optimization progress \cite{Sutton2018Reinforcement, Bai2023Evolutionary}.
More broadly, RL-based online control has also been studied in other population-based optimizers, including policy-gradient control in differential evolution and PPO-based parameter adaptation in iSOMA \cite{Sun2021Learning, Klein2024Optimizing}.

Existing RL-PSO methods differ mainly along two design axes: how the state is summarized and how control is distributed across the swarm.
Value-based approaches discretize the action space and typically use RL for strategy selection rather than direct coefficient generation \cite{Watkins1992Qlearning}.
MPSORL \cite{Meng2023Multistrategy} uses non-uniform fitness-grade states to choose among predefined PSO strategies, DPQ-PSO \cite{Aoun2024Deep} combines DQN with Hidden Markov state inference, and RLNDM-PSO \cite{Li2023Reinforcement} classifies particles into four search states and switches among predefined velocity-generation behaviors.
In all three cases, RL chooses among discrete behaviors instead of distributing continuous parameters across particles.

Continuous-control approaches replace discrete strategy selection with actor-critic adaptation.
RLAM-PSO \cite{Yin2023RLAMPSO} uses DDPG with a three-feature snapshot state built from progress, diversity, and no-improvement duration, and emits continuous parameter sets for fixed particle subgroups.
DDPGPSO \cite{Zhang2024DDPGPSO} likewise uses DDPG for continuous parameter control.
SAC-SAPSO \cite{vonEschwege2024Soft} adopts entropy-regularized SAC, but still outputs one shared 3D coefficient triplet for the whole swarm from snapshot observations such as particle velocities and global feasibility and stability statistics.
HRLPSO \cite{Liu2023HRLPSO} extends RL control to a broader hybrid adaptation scheme.

Taken together, this literature shows clear progress in RL backbone choice, but provides much weaker causal evidence about control-interface design.
Prior studies typically commit to one interface a priori, such as discrete strategy control, fixed-subgroup continuous control, a single global coefficient triplet, or a broader hybrid controller, and then compare end-to-end algorithms that also differ in backbone, state design, and training setup.
As a result, the literature currently provides little controlled evidence on whether and how control granularity itself affects performance under a matched RL backbone.
State design and reward specification remain important secondary choices, but the most immediate unresolved issue is that existing comparisons do not cleanly separate the benefit of the RL algorithm from the benefit of the interface through which control is distributed across particles.
In particular, the literature still does not establish under matched learning conditions whether coarse global control, fixed subgroup structure, or high-dimensional direct particle-wise control is the more effective interface for RL-guided PSO.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
We consider the problem of planning a collision-free trajectory for a UAV operating in a 3D environment.
The environment is represented by a digital elevation map (DEM) $\mathcal{E}: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ that returns terrain height for any $(x, y)$ coordinate, augmented with a set of cylindrical no-fly zones $\mathcal{O} = \{(c_j, r_j, h_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ representing threat areas.
The UAV must navigate from a start position $s_{\text{start}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ to a goal position $s_{\text{goal}} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ while minimizing the fitness function defined in Equation~\ref{eq:fitness}.
\begin{figure}[!htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{data/ProblemFormulation.pdf}
\caption{Geometric definition of UAV path planning parameters}
\label{fig:problem_formulation}
\end{figure}
A candidate path is encoded as a sequence of $n$ waypoints $P = [p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n]$ where each $p_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i) \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
The UAV follows linear segments between consecutive waypoints, yielding total path length:
\begin{equation}
L(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \|p_{i+1} - p_i\|_2
\label{eq:path_length}
\end{equation}
Path quality is evaluated through a weighted fitness function that balances competing objectives:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
f(P) ={}& \lambda_1 L(P) + \lambda_2 C_{\text{turn}}(P) + \lambda_3 C_{\text{climb}}(P) \\
&+ \lambda_4 C_{\text{alt}}(P) + C_{\text{safety}}(P)
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
The turn penalty $C_{\text{turn}}$ applies a linear penalty for directional changes that exceed a minimal tolerance threshold:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{turn}}(P) = \sum_{i=2}^{n-1} \max\left(0, \theta_i - \theta_{\max}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ is the turn angle between consecutive segments $i-1$ and $i$, and $\theta_{\max}$ is the maximum zero-penalty turn threshold (set to $\symThetaTol$ rad).
The climb penalty $C_{\text{climb}}$ penalizes both steep climb adjustments and absolute climb angles:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{climb}}(P) = \sum_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( 2|\xi_i - \xi_{i-1}| + |\xi_i| + |\xi_{i-1}| \right)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\xi_i = \arctan\left(\frac{z_{i+1} - z_i}{\|p_{i+1} - p_i\|_{xy}}\right)$ is the climb angle of the $i$-th segment. The safety penalty $C_{\text{safety}}$ acts as an absolute constraint combining terrain, obstacle, and path feasibility violations (applying infinite or heavy penalties for penetrating restricted boundaries):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\text{safety}}(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Bigl[ &\max(0, \mathcal{E}(x_i, y_i) + h_{\min} - z_i) \\
&+ \sum_{j=1}^{M} \max(0, r_j - d_{ij}) \Bigr]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij}$ is the distance from waypoint $i$ to obstacle $j$.
The altitude cost $C_{\text{alt}}$ encourages the UAV to maintain low-level flight for terrain masking and energy efficiency:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{alt}}(P) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i
\end{equation}
The normalized weights $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_4$ balance efficiency against soft constraints.
The safety term $C_{\text{safety}}$ dominates the objective to enforce strict feasibility.
Lower fitness values indicate better paths.
\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
The RL-based PSO parameter adaptation problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
In the proposed algorithm, the MDP is defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{R}, \mathcal{T}, \symGamma)$ as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mdp_components}.
The state space $\mathcal{S}$ encodes compact swarm statistics over a short temporal window.
The action space $\mathcal{A}$ is a $\symActiondim$-D latent control vector which is expanded into particle-wise PSO coefficients through the rank-residual mapping.
The reward function $\mathcal{R}$ provides direct feedback based on whether the swarm improves the current best fitness.
The transition rules $\mathcal{T}$ describe deterministic updates computed via the standard PSO velocity and position equations, functioning effectively as the environment transition function during learning.
The discount factor $\symGamma$ determines the temporal preference for cumulative returns.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Formulation}
To address the limitations of static state observations used in prior RL-PSO methods, we adopt a compact swarm-statistics state representation. This follows state-representation literature, which treats the state as a compact depiction of observations that preserves the information necessary for control and often benefits from temporal continuity or predictive dynamics \cite{Lesort2018State}.
At each iteration, the controller extracts nine normalized swarm-level features $f_1, \dots, f_9 \in [0,1]$ summarizing progress, diversity, fitness, constraints, and swarm kinematics. The exact mathematical formulation for each feature is detailed in Table~\ref{tab:state_features}.

These features are buffered over a temporal window of $\symWindowsize$ iterations.
In the retained benchmarked configuration, the deployed encoder is intentionally lightweight: it forms a $\symStatedim$-D state by concatenating the current nine features, the first three coordinates of the temporal mean, and the first three coordinates of the one-step trend.
This keeps the controller online-feasible while still exposing short-horizon temporal context.
To ensure stable critic conditioning, all features are clipped to $[0,1]$.
In the mathematical formulations detailed in Table~\ref{tab:state_features}, let $x_{i,d}$ and $v_{i}$ denote the position and velocity of particle $i$, $g$ the global best position, $F$ the set of valid particle fitnesses, $\symMaxIters$ the maximum iterations, $\symPopsize$ the swarm size, $D$ the problem dimension, and $\Delta_d = \max_i x_{i,d} - \min_i x_{i,d}$.

The retained controller uses a direct fitness-improvement reward. Let $\Delta f = f_{\text{gbest}}^{t-1} - f_{\text{gbest}}^{t}$. The reward is defined as:
\begin{equation}
r_t =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } \Delta f > 0 \\
-1, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:reward_total}
\end{equation}
This design keeps the reinforcement signal aligned with the optimization objective while avoiding auxiliary shaping terms.
We do not claim that shaping is inherently problematic: potential-based shaping can preserve optimal policies \cite{Ng1999Policy}, but the retained benchmark uses the direct reward to keep the learning signal transparent and easy to attribute.

RRSACPSO employs Soft Actor-Critic \cite{Haarnoja2018Soft} to learn the parameter-adaptation policy.
The SAC objective is the entropy-regularized expected return:
\begin{equation}
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \left(r_t + \alpha \mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_t))\right)\right]
\label{eq:sac_objective}
\end{equation} where $\alpha$ is the temperature parameter (automatically tuned) and $\mathcal{H}$ is the policy entropy.
The algorithm maintains three components: Actor Network $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})$: A Gaussian policy with learnable mean and diagonal covariance. In the retained implementation the actor is a compact MLP operating on the flat 15D state vector. Actions are sampled via reparameterization:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_t = \tanh(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t) \odot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}), \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})
\end{equation} Twin Critic Networks $Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}$: Two independent scalar Q-functions are retained together with target networks. The critic loss minimizes the Bellman error using the minimum of both critics:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_Q(\phi_i) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},r,\mathbf{s}')} \Bigl[ \bigl(Q_{\phi_i}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}) - (&r + \symGamma \min_{j=1,2} Q_{\phi_j}(\mathbf{s}', \mathbf{a}') \\
&- \alpha \log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}'|\mathbf{s}') )\bigr)^2 \Bigr]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation} Temperature Parameter $\alpha$: Automatically adjusted to match a target entropy $\bar{\mathcal{H}} = -\dim(\mathcal{A}) = -\symActiondim$ via:
\begin{equation}
L_\alpha = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s} \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[-\alpha \left(\log \pi_\theta(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s}) + \bar{\mathcal{H}}\right)\right]
\end{equation} In the retained benchmarked configuration, target networks remain enabled, automatic entropy tuning is active, and the critics are standard twin scalar Q-networks; TQC, CrossQ, and critic batch normalization are disabled.

\subsection{Rank-Residual Parameter Expansion}
The rank-residual parameterization is the core mechanism linking the SAC controller to particle-wise PSO adaptation.
Like per-particle adaptive PSO schemes such as SAEPSO and UAPSO \cite{Liu2025A, Isiet2019Selfadapting}, the expansion conditions each particle's coefficients on its current search status.
However, instead of hand-crafting the coefficient rules directly, RRSACPSO learns a shared latent control vector $\mathbf{a}_t = [a_1,\dots,a_9]^\top \in [-1,1]^9$ and converts it into particle-wise residuals around a deterministic baseline schedule. Inspired by Residual Reinforcement Learning \cite{Johannink2019Residual}, which stabilizes learning by superimposing an RL policy over a baseline controller, this approach ensures stable search behavior while allowing fine-grained corrections.
With normalized progress $\rho_t = \min\!\left(1,\max\!\left(0,\frac{t-1}{T}\right)\right)$, the baseline coefficients are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_t^{\mathrm{base}} &= w_{\max}^{\mathrm{sch}} - \left(w_{\max}^{\mathrm{sch}} - w_{\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}\right)\rho_t^{\symExpInertia}, \\
c_{1,t}^{\mathrm{base}} &= c_{1,\max}^{\mathrm{sch}} - \left(c_{1,\max}^{\mathrm{sch}} - c_{1,\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}\right)\rho_t, \\
c_{2,t}^{\mathrm{base}} &= c_{2,\min}^{\mathrm{sch}} + \left(c_{2,\max}^{\mathrm{sch}} - c_{2,\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}\right)\rho_t,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rank_residual_base}
\end{equation}
where the retained benchmark uses $(w_{\max}^{\mathrm{sch}}, w_{\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}) = (\symWmaxSch, \symWminSch)$ and $(c_{1,\max}^{\mathrm{sch}}, c_{1,\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}) = (c_{2,\max}^{\mathrm{sch}}, c_{2,\min}^{\mathrm{sch}}) = (\symCmaxSch, \symCminSch)$.

For particle $i$, three centered context signals are computed:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_{i,t} &= \frac{\operatorname{rank}_{i,t}}{N-1} - 0.5, \\
u_{i,t} &= \tanh\!\left(\frac{\|v_{i,t}\|_2 - \mu_v}{\sigma_v + 10^{-8}}\right), \\
s_{i,t} &= \min\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{stag}_{i,t}}{\symStagMaxPart}, 1\right) - 0.5,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rank_residual_context}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{rank}_{i,t}$ is assigned from best to worst particle according to personal-best fitness, so the best particle has $r_{i,t}=-0.5$ and the worst particle has $r_{i,t}=0.5$, while $\mu_v$ and $\sigma_v$ are the swarm mean and standard deviation of velocity magnitude.

The latent action is then expanded into residual updates. The retained residual gains are chosen to keep the learned policy in a correction role around the baseline schedule rather than to replace it outright: $\symResWeightW$ keeps inertia perturbations small relative to the admissible interval $[\symWclipMin,\symWclipMax]$, while $\symResWeightC$ allows larger but still bounded shifts of $c_1$ and $c_2$ within $[\symCclipMin,\symCclipMax]$. 

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta w_{i,t} &= \symResWeightW\left(a_1 + a_2 r_{i,t} + a_3 u_{i,t}\right), \\
\Delta c_{1,i,t} &= \symResWeightC\left(a_4 + a_5 s_{i,t} + a_6 r_{i,t}\right), \\
\Delta c_{2,i,t} &= \symResWeightC\left(a_7 - a_8 r_{i,t} - a_9 s_{i,t}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rank_residual_delta}
\end{equation}
and the final PSO coefficients are obtained by clipping to the admissible ranges
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_{i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}_{[\symWclipMin,\,\symWclipMax]}\!\left(w_t^{\mathrm{base}} + \Delta w_{i,t}\right), \\
c_{1,i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}_{[\symCclipMin,\,\symCclipMax]}\!\left(c_{1,t}^{\mathrm{base}} + \Delta c_{1,i,t}\right), \\
c_{2,i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}_{[\symCclipMin,\,\symCclipMax]}\!\left(c_{2,t}^{\mathrm{base}} + \Delta c_{2,i,t}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rank_residual_final}
\end{equation}
This design preserves a low-dimensional RL control problem while still allowing heterogeneous parameter assignments across the swarm.
Poorly performing or stagnating particles can therefore be pushed toward more exploratory settings, while better-ranked particles remain closer to exploitative coefficients around the current best region.
The mapping keeps the controller online-feasible and avoids the dimensionality explosion that would arise if the policy directly emitted all $3\symPopsize$ particle parameters independently.

\subsection{Algorithm Workflow}
The complete integration of the retained SAC agent with the PSO search process is summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:apexpso}.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RRSACPSO Parameter Adaptation}
\label{alg:apexpso}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Swarm size $\symPopsize$, max iterations $\symMaxIters$, replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$
\State \textbf{Initialize:} Swarm positions $x_i^0$, velocities $v_i^0$, personal bests $p_i^0$, global best $g^0$
\State \textbf{Initialize:} Actor $\pi_\theta$, critics $Q_{\phi_{1,2}}$, temperature $\alpha$
\For{$t = 1$ to $\symMaxIters$}
\State Compute compact swarm statistics $s_t$
\State Sample latent action $\mathbf{a}_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t)$
\State Expand $\mathbf{a}_t$ through rank-residual control to particle-wise PSO coefficients $\{w_{i,t}, c_{1,i,t}, c_{2,i,t}\}_{i=1}^{\symPopsize}$
\For{each particle $i \in \{1, \dots, \symPopsize\}$}
\State Update velocity $v_i^{t} \leftarrow w_{i,t} v_i^{t-1} + c_{1,i,t} r_1 (p_i^{t-1} - x_i^{t-1}) + c_{2,i,t} r_2 (g^{t-1} - x_i^{t-1})$
\State Update position $x_i^{t} \leftarrow x_i^{t-1} + v_i^{t}$
\EndFor
\State Evaluate fitness $f(x_i^t)$ and update $p_i^t$ and $g^t$
\State Compute fitness-improvement reward $r_t$ (Eq. \ref{eq:reward_total})
\State Observe $s_{t+1}$ and store transition $(s_t, \mathbf{a}_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
\If{training session active}
\State Sample minibatch $\mathcal{B} \sim \mathcal{D}$ and update critics, actor, and temperature
\EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return Optimal path $g^t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}
Path quality is measured by global best fitness (Equation \ref{eq:fitness}), which combines path length, turning penalties, climbing penalties, and collision penalties.
Lower fitness indicates better paths.
The main benchmark evaluates the retained RRSACPSO configuration in online rank-residual mode (9D latent action) against RL-based and classical PSO baselines.
These are compared against a broad range of traditional and adaptive PSO variants, including PSO-LDIW, FIPS, CLPSO, SAEPSO, and UAPSO.
The RL baselines are RLAM-PSO (DDPG) \cite{Yin2023RLAMPSO}, DQN-PSO \cite{Aoun2024Deep}, SAC-SAPSO \cite{vonEschwege2024Soft}, MPSORL, and PPO-PSO.
The benchmark claims in this paper focus on the retained plain-SAC plus rank-residual configuration described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}.
For each algorithm, we conduct $\symNumruns$ independent runs per scenario and report mean and standard deviation on the same endpoint used for optimization (the total fitness objective).
Inferential analysis uses two-sided paired t-tests as the primary test, with two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as a robustness check.
Within each scenario, all pairwise p-values are adjusted using Holm--Bonferroni family-wise correction.
For each pair, we additionally report the paired mean difference with a 95\% confidence interval, Cohen's $d_z$, and rank-biserial correlation.

The experimental protocol is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Evaluation environment:} The primary fixed-map benchmark uses map size $[100,100,100]$, start point $[10,95,10]$, goal point $[97,2,10]$, and three scenarios with DEM map with 0, 5, and 10 danger zones. The RL comparison additionally reports a fourth cross-terrain scenario on terrainStruct c100 with zero danger zones and the terrain-resolved scenario geometry.
\item \textbf{Deterministic paired-seed policy:} For run $r$ and scenario $s$, a common base seed $1000r + 100s$ is used across all algorithms, enabling paired statistical comparison under matched stochastic conditions.
\item \textbf{Benchmark budget:} Each algorithm is evaluated in a single direct global-planning run with 100 particles, 1000 iterations, and 30 runs per scenario.
\item \textbf{Normalized baseline configuration:} Baselines are compared under the same evaluation protocol while preserving each method's core update equations and state-action design. Hyperparameters are fixed before evaluation and are not retuned per scenario.
\item \textbf{RRSACPSO setup:} Experiments use online rank-residual mode with a 9D latent action expanded into particle-wise PSO coefficients. The reported setup uses SAC with entropy tuning, target networks, and twin critics.
\item \textbf{Statistical reporting:} Each table reports mean and standard deviation over 30 independent runs per scenario on per-run global fitness. Pairwise inference uses two-sided paired t-tests (primary) and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (robustness), with Holm--Bonferroni correction within each scenario. We also report corrected p-values, 95\% confidence intervals for paired mean differences, and effect sizes ($d_z$, rank-biserial $r$).
\end{enumerate}
All results reported in Tables~\ref{tab:rl_comparison} and \ref{tab:others_comparison} follow this fairness protocol.

The parameter-tracking panel provides direct evidence that RRSACPSO uses a wide adaptive range for $w$, $c_1$, and $c_2$, unlike the flatter PPO-PSO trace and the more step-like SAC-SAPSO and MPSORL traces.

\subsection{SAC Control Granularity and Rank-Residual Ablation}
\label{subsec:sac_granularity_ablation_results}
To probe the role of the control interface, we ran an internal four-mode ablation under the same retained SAC backbone used throughout this paper.
All four modes use the same 15D temporal-summary state, binary fitness-improvement reward, twin-critic target-network training, and paired-seed protocol, with only the action-to-parameter mapping changed.
Within this subsection, `SACPSO-Global', `SACPSO-5Subgroup', and `SACPSO-PerParticle' are matched internal baselines and should not be confused with the external SAC-SAPSO method in Table~\ref{tab:rl_comparison}.
`SACPSO-Global' applies one coefficient triplet to the full swarm, `SACPSO-5Subgroup' assigns one triplet per fixed subgroup, and `SACPSO-PerParticle' outputs direct particle-wise coefficients.
RRSACPSO retains the same plain-SAC controller but expands a compact 9D latent action into particle-wise coefficients through rank-residual modulation around the deterministic baseline schedule, whereas the three internal baselines map their actions directly to coefficient values.
For the current 100-particle benchmark, these modes correspond to 3D, 15D, 300D, and 9D latent-action control spaces, respectively.

Table~\ref{tab:sac_granularity_ablation} shows that RRSACPSO achieves the lowest mean fitness in all four scenarios.
The clearest gains appear in the three first scenarios, where RRSACPSO significantly outperforms all three matched baselines in Scenarios 1 and 3 and remains significantly better than the global and five-subgroup variants in Scenario 2.
On the cross-terrain c100 scenario, RRSACPSO still has the best mean fitness, but the pairwise differences are not significant after Holm correction, so that fourth-scenario result should be interpreted as directional rather than definitive.

\subsection{Results Summary}
The matched benchmark across the four-scenario RL comparison and the four-scenario classical comparison yields several main observations.
Within the internal same-backbone ablation, RRSACPSO outperforms SACPSO-Global, SACPSO-5Subgroup, and SACPSO-PerParticle on mean fitness in all four scenarios, with significant advantages on the three fixed-map cases and only directional gains on the cross-terrain c100 case.
Among RL-based external controllers, PPO-PSO achieves the best mean fitness in Scenarios 1, 2, and 4, while MPSORL is best in Scenario 3.
RRSACPSO shows the lowest variance in Scenarios 1 and 2 and is not significantly different from PPO-PSO or MPSORL on the cross-terrain Scenario 4.
Against classical and adaptive PSO methods, SAEPSO achieves the best mean fitness in Scenarios 1 and 4 while SAEPSO* achieves the best mean fitness in Scenarios 2 and 3, with PSO-LDIW also recording a lower mean than RRSACPSO in Scenario 1.
RRSACPSO consistently outperforms weaker baselines such as CLPSO and usually improves upon DQN-PSO and SAC-SAPSO, but it does not dominate the stronger PPO-PSO, MPSORL, and SAEPSO-family baselines in the present benchmark.
Taken together, the tables and representative figures support a narrower conclusion: the maximum-entropy SAC objective combined with the rank-residual parameterization produces visibly richer parameter dynamics, but its current benefit is selective rather than universal.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The experimental results show that RRSACPSO is a competitive adaptive-control formulation for PSO-based UAV path planning, although it is not the strongest overall method on the current matched benchmark.
This section interprets the main findings, discusses the principal limitations, and situates the method within the broader literature.
\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
The benchmark evidence is consistent with the retained rank-residual controller being the main distinguishing mechanism of the current method.
This interpretation can be framed in the control-interface design space summarized in Section~\ref{sec:background}, rather than only in terms of the present implementation.
Prior RL-PSO studies largely choose one control interface in advance: RLAM-PSO uses fixed-subgroup continuous control, SAC-SAPSO uses a single global coefficient triplet, and MPSORL or RLNDM-PSO rely on discrete strategy selection \cite{Yin2023RLAMPSO, vonEschwege2024Soft, Meng2023Multistrategy, Li2023Reinforcement}.
Under that literature framing, the main result here is not simply that SAC can control PSO parameters, but that a compact latent interface with structured expansion can outperform matched global, subgroup, and direct per-particle interfaces under the same SAC backbone.

This reading is also consistent with broader literature on adaptive parameter control.
Classical adaptive methods such as APSO, SAEPSO, and UAPSO improve performance by responding to online swarm state rather than relying on static coefficients \cite{Zhan2009Adaptive, Liu2025A, Isiet2019Selfadapting}.
RRSACPSO fits within that state-aware tradition, but replaces hand-designed adaptation laws with a learned low-dimensional control signal whose output is still constrained by an explicit schedule.
The resulting behavior in Figure~\ref{fig:rl_diagnostics} and Figure~\ref{fig:others_convergence_tracking} is therefore most naturally interpreted as learned state-aware modulation around a prior, not as unconstrained coefficient generation.

The same-backbone ablation sharpens that point.
Across the three fixed-map scenarios, the matched global and five-subgroup variants are significantly worse, while the direct per-particle controller increases action dimension without recovering the same behavior.
That pattern is consistent with the concern in RL control that larger action spaces can worsen credit assignment and sample efficiency \cite{DulacArnold2015Deep, Zhou2020PLAS}.
In other words, the ablation does not only favor more heterogeneity; it favors a particular way of introducing heterogeneity through structured, low-dimensional control.

At the same time, the external benchmark indicates that PPO-PSO and MPSORL achieve lower mean fitness than RRSACPSO in the harder fixed-map RL scenarios, while the cross-terrain fourth scenario leaves RRSACPSO statistically tied with both.
This places the present contribution in a narrower literature position.
The evidence supports rank-residual control as an effective interface within SAC-based PSO adaptation, but not as a universal replacement for all alternative RL controllers or for the strongest hand-crafted adaptive PSO methods.
\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment.
First, the retained online controller still requires hyperparameter tuning and introduces additional compute during each run, which may not be feasible for rapidly changing mission profiles.
Second, our evaluation focused on static terrain maps; dynamic obstacles and time-varying threat zones remain untested.
Third, while the policy is not significantly different from PPO-PSO or MPSORL on the fourth cross-terrain scenario, transfer to substantially different terrain families (e.g., urban canyons, open water, or dynamic maritime scenes) was not evaluated.
Fourth, the fitness function weights ($\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_5$) were hand-tuned; automatic weight adaptation could further improve performance.
Fifth, the retained binary reward design is intentionally simple; alternative reward-shaping strategies were not explored in the current repository.
Finally, the retained SAC-style controller still introduces non-trivial online training overhead within each run, and the present benchmark does not show a consistent wall-clock advantage over the strongest hand-crafted adaptive baselines.
\subsection{Control Granularity and Rank-Residual Ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:sac_granularity_ablation} clarifies the same-backbone story.
Across the three first scenarios, RRSACPSO achieves the best mean fitness in all three comparisons, and the strongest ablation signal comes from SACPSO-Global and SACPSO-5Subgroup, both of which are significantly worse in every fixed-map case.
SACPSO-PerParticle narrows the gap in Scenario 2, but even there it remains directionally worse after Holm correction, so simply increasing the control dimension from a structured 9D latent action to direct 300D control does not recover the behavior of rank-residual control.

Among these variants, SACPSO-Global is the most interpretable internal baseline because it keeps the retained plain-SAC backbone but replaces the rank-residual scheduled interface with one directly controlled global coefficient triplet.
Its consistent degradation on Scenarios 1--3, together with its directional degradation on Scenario 4, is the strongest evidence that the main retained contribution is not SAC alone but the structured mapping from a compact 9D latent action to particle-wise PSO coefficients around a scheduled prior.

SACPSO-5Subgroup and SACPSO-PerParticle show that heterogeneity by itself is not sufficient unless it is well structured.
The five-subgroup variant remains worse than RRSACPSO on every scenario, which suggests that fixed partitions are too coarse to track each particle's evolving search role.
The fully per-particle controller is more expressive, but it is also the slowest variant in all four scenarios and still underperforms RRSACPSO on mean fitness, consistent with harder credit assignment in a much larger continuous action space \cite{DulacArnold2015Deep, Zhou2020PLAS}.
Because these internal baselines directly emit coefficient values while RRSACPSO applies residual corrections around a deterministic schedule, this ablation should be read as evidence for the overall control-interface design rather than as a pure granularity-only test.
On the cross-terrain c100 case, RRSACPSO again has the best mean fitness, but the differences are not significant, so the cross-terrain evidence should be read as supportive rather than conclusive.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented RRSACPSO, a rank-residual RL framework for adaptive PSO parameter control in UAV path planning.
The method combines a compact swarm-summary state, a direct fitness-improvement reward, and a retained SAC backbone with a control law that expands a 9D latent action into particle-wise inertia, cognitive, and social coefficients.
The main validated result is not universal benchmark leadership, but that structured low-dimensional rank-residual control performs better than matched global, fixed-subgroup, or direct per-particle SAC control under the same backbone.
In the internal four-mode ablation, RRSACPSO achieves the best mean fitness in all four scenarios, with significant gains on the three fixed-map cases.
Against external RL baselines, it records the lowest variance in the first two fixed-map scenarios but does not achieve the best mean fitness against PPO-PSO or MPSORL in the harder RL cases, and against strong classical baselines it does not establish a consistent wall-clock advantage.
Within the broader RL-PSO literature, these results support RRSACPSO as evidence that control-interface design matters alongside RL-backbone choice, especially when particle-wise heterogeneity is desired without paying the full action-space cost of direct per-particle control.
Future work should systematically investigate hyperparameter sensitivity for the retained controller, extend the state representation to natively handle dynamic obstacles, and reduce online adaptation cost across broader operational domains.
